'''Raja Birbal''' (born '''Mahesh Das'''; 1528 – 16 February 1586) was a senior advisor, poet, and military commander in the Mughal court under Emperor [[Akbar]].<ref name="auto4">{{cite encyclopedia |title=Bīrbal |encyclopedia=Encyclopædia Britannica |url=https://www.britannica.com/biography/Birbal |access-date=17 November 2026}}</ref> Appointed to the Mughal court between 1556 and 1562, he rose to become one of Akbar's principal advisers and was granted the title ''Kavi Rai'' (poet laureate). He was also the only Hindu known to have adopted [[Din-i Ilahi]], the syncretic religious movement founded by the emperor.<ref name="auto2">{{cite book |last=Richards |first=John F. |title=The Mughal Empire |series=The New Cambridge History of India |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=1993 |isbn=978-0-521-56603-2 |pages=40–45}}</ref> Birbal was killed on 16 February 1586 when [[Yusufzai]] tribesmen ambushed his forces in the [[Battle of the Malandari Pass|Malandari Pass]] during a Mughal campaign in the northwest.<ref name="auto4"/>

== Early life and entry into Akbar's court ==
Mahesh Das was born in 1528 in the vicinity of [[Kalpi]] (in modern-day [[Uttar Pradesh]]) into a [[Brahman]] family with a background in poetry and literature.<ref name="auto4"/> Trained in [[Hindi]], [[Sanskrit]], and [[Persian language|Persian]], he wrote poetry and music in [[Braj Bhasha]], beginning his career at the court of [[Raja Ram Chandra]] of [[Rewa, Madhya Pradesh|Rewa]] under the pen name ''Brahma Kavi''.<ref name="auto5">{{cite book |last=Busch |first=Allison |title=Poetry of Kings: The Classical Hindi Literature of Mughal India |publisher=Oxford University Press |year=2011 |isbn=978-0-19-976592-8 |pages=35–38}}</ref>

Between 1556 and 1562, he joined Akbar's imperial service, where his literary skill and conversational wit won him favor.<ref name="auto1">{{cite book |last=Hodivala |first=Shahpurshah Hormasji |title=Studies in Indo-Muslim History: A Critical Commentary on Elliot and Dowson's History of India as Told by Its Own Historians |publisher=Popular Book Depot |year=1939 |pages=558–560}}</ref> Akbar granted him the title of ''Raja'' and the name ''Birbal'', which historians derive from ''Vir Var'', a loyal courtier in the Sanskrit story collection ''[[Vetala Panchavimshati]]''.<ref name="auto1"/>

== Administrative and military career ==
Within the Mughal administration, Birbal served as a [[mansabdar]] with both civil and military duties, assisting Akbar with domestic governance and imperial correspondence.<ref name="auto2"/> He participated in the 1572 expedition to [[Punjab]] against [[Mirza Muhammad Hakim]] and accompanied Akbar on military campaigns in [[Gujarat]]. He accompanied Akbar on military campaigns in [[Gujarat]] and participated in the 1572 expedition to [[Punjab]] against [[Mirza Muhammad Hakim]].<ref name="auto3">{{cite book |last=Abu'l-Fazl ibn Mubarak |translator-last=Beveridge |translator-first=Henry |title=The Akbarnama of Abu'l-Fazl |volume=3 |publisher=Asiatic Society of Bengal |year=1907 |pages=731–735}}</ref>

In February 1586, Akbar dispatched Birbal with reinforcements to the northwestern frontier (modern -day [[Swat District]], [[Khyber Pakhtunkhwa]], Pakistan) to suppress a rebellion by the [[Yusufzai]]  tribes.<ref name="auto3"/> During an ambush at the [[Battle of Malandari Pass|Malandari Pass]], he was killed alongside several thousand Mughal troops.<ref>{{cite book |last=Richards |first=John F. |title=The Mughal Empire |series=The New Cambridge History of India |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=1993 |isbn=978-0-521-56603-2 |pages=50–51}}</ref> Upon learning of Birbal's death, Akbar abstained from food and drink for two days, grieving that the body could not be recovered for Hindu funeral rites.<ref>{{cite book |last=Abu'l-Fazl ibn Mubarak |translator-last=Beveridge |translator-first=Henry |title=The Akbarnama of Abu'l-Fazl |volume=3 |publisher=Asiatic Society of Bengal |year=1907 |pages=735–736}}</ref>

== Historical record and tradition ==
Contemporary chroniclers such as [[Abu'l-Fazl ibn Mubarak]] and [[Abd al-Qadir Badayuni|Badayuni]] documented Birbal's standing as a court poet, his close personal standing with Akbar, and his adherence to the emperor's policy of ''[[Sulh-i-kul]]'' (universal peace).<ref name="auto5"/> Although orthodox courtiers resented his influence, historians conclude that Birbal merely reinforced, rather than originated, Akbar's religiously inclusive policies.<ref name="auto4"/> 

After his death, Birbal’s historical career was largely overshadowed in [[Indian folklore]] by oral tales depicting him as a witty courtier who regularly outsmarted his peers and the emperor.<ref name="auto">{{cite book |last=Otto |first=Beatrice K. |title=Fools Are Everywhere: The Court Jester Around the World |publisher=University of Chicago Press |year=2001 |isbn=978-0-226-64091-4 |pages=12–15}}</ref> A two-storey red sandstone palace at [[Fatehpur Sikri]] is popularly known as Birbal's House, though architectural historians attribute its construction to Akbar's queens and consider it unlikely that he resided there.<ref name="auto4"/><ref>{{cite book |last=Asher |first=Catherine B. |title=Architecture of Mughal India |series=The New Cambridge History of India |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=1992 |isbn=978-0-521-26728-1 |pages=58–60}}</ref>

== Legacy and popular culture ==
By Akbar's death, oral tales of Birbal's wit and moral judgment were widespread in northern India, gradually merging with older [[Sanskrit]], [[Persian language|Persian]], and [[Arabic]] wisdom tales.<ref>{{cite book |last=Naim |first=C. M. |title=Urdu Texts and Contexts: The Selected Essays of C. M. Naim |publisher=Permanent Black |year=2004 |isbn=978-81-7824-075-6 |pages=122–125}}</ref> Within the folktale tradition, Birbal functions as a trickster figure whose wit and sharp reasoning expose the hypocrisy of corrupt officials and orthodox clerics.<ref name="auto"/>

The Akbar–Birbal folktales remain widely circulated in modern [[South Asia]] through children's literature, comic books such as ''[[Amar Chitra Katha]]'', and television adaptations.<ref name="auto4"/>
